# Do electric motorcycles pay better than petrol for Nairobi boda boda riders?

**The question.** Electric motorcycles cost more upfront, and e-mobility operators want to know whether riders earn enough on them to justify the switch, and if not, why.

**The answer.** Keep electrifying, but put the next shilling into getting electric riders to drive further, not into cheaper energy or maintenance. Per kilometre, electric riders already make KES 11.18 against KES 11.68 for petrol, and the KES 159 daily profit gap (KES 837 vs 996) comes from electric riders driving 75 km a day against 85 km. If that shorter distance turns out to be a hard limit, such as battery range, electric will stay behind and the upfront premium will not pay back. Before the next fleet purchase, I would ask the operator for swap-station and charging records for the tracked electric riders, so the cause of the shorter distance can be found.

**How I got there.** I compared 120 riders using GPS trip records and daily financial surveys: profit per day, profit per kilometre, running costs per kilometre and distance driven. Where survey figures were inconsistent, such as zero revenue on a 90 km trip, I corrected them using fuel-consumption benchmarks and published battery-swap prices instead of deleting them.

**What I'd do next.** Find out why electric riders drive less. It could be battery range, swap-station access, route assignment, or a learning curve, and each one needs a different fix. Until then, compare fleets on profit per kilometre rather than daily profit, because daily profit depends on how much a rider chooses to drive.

**Caveats.** Only 9 electric motorcycles were tracked against more than 100 petrol ones, so treat the findings as directional. Revenue and costs come from phone surveys and were partly corrected by estimate, and a few extreme values remain in the petrol group (as low as -KES 8,800 a day). The data covers Nairobi only, from a 2023 study.roups.
